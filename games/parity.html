Why this is the one table the house can't win

Buy a call and sell a put at the same strike and expiry, and whatever the stock does you end up owning it at K. That is the stock, bought on credit. So the two must cost the same today:

C − P = S − K·e−rT

Nothing in that line is a forecast. It does not care what volatility is, what the stock is likely to do, or whether Black–Scholes is true. If the two sides disagree by more than it costs to trade, you take the difference and hold a position that nets to exactly zero at expiry — which is why, in a real market, they don't disagree for long.

When the call side is rich you sell the call, buy the put, buy the stock, and borrow the discounted strike — a conversion. When it's cheap you do the reverse and lend instead — a reversal. Either way the gap is yours on day one.

The quotes on this table are generated with Black–Scholes so the board looks like a real one, but the thing being scored never touches it. Parity is an invariant, not a model — the same kind of claim as a ledger that must sum to zero, or a write that must survive a crash. Finding the board where it has quietly stopped holding is the whole job.

Boards are called fair inside a 5¢ tolerance, since quotes are rounded to the cent.
